A small guide · 4 minute read

What a shadow
can’t tell you.

Three temperatures often get bundled into one heat headline. Here’s what each one measures — and what to look for before drawing a conclusion.

Still / Life research deskSources linked below10 November 2026
A QUICK GLOSSARY Surface temperature Air temperature Human exposure How to read a claim

Stand in the shade on a hot afternoon and the difference can feel immediate. But “the shade is cooler” leaves a crucial question unanswered: cooler what? A surface, the air around it, or the conditions a person’s body is experiencing?

Those are separate things. Public agencies use different methods to measure them, and a number without its method can make unlike observations look comparable.

1. Surface temperature

This is the temperature of a material — a roof, road, wall, or patch of soil. It can change sharply with sun exposure, color, moisture, and material. The EPA notes that conventional roofing materials on a warm day can be as much as 66°F hotter than surrounding air. That figure describes a possible surface-to-air difference, not a claim that every roof or nearby person is 66°F hotter.

Satellite or infrared measurements can map surface temperatures over large areas. They are valuable for identifying hot roofs or paved areas, but they do not directly measure the air people breathe. Compare a surface-temperature map with another surface-temperature map, not with a weather-station air reading as though the methods were the same.

2. Air temperature

Air temperature is the familiar thermometer reading. In its overview of U.S. research, the EPA says urban areas are typically 1–7°F warmer during daytime and 2–5°F warmer at night than outlying areas. These are broad ranges across locations and studies. A reading from one instrument at one height and place cannot represent every block of a city.

Weather, wind, cloud cover, building layout, pavement, and vegetation all affect local conditions. That is why a good comparison needs context: where the measurements were taken, when they were taken, and how the comparison areas were chosen.

3. Heat exposure

A person’s heat burden involves more than air temperature. The WHO describes the body’s heat balance as shaped by the environment — including humidity, wind, and thermal radiation — alongside clothing, activity, and the body’s ability to shed heat. Direct sun can add radiant heat; shade can block much of that direct exposure even when the air temperature changes little.

Risk also depends on who is exposed and for how long. The WHO identifies outdoor and manual workers as exposed through their work, and notes that people with lower-quality housing or limited access to cooling can face disproportionate overheating. There is no single shadow measurement that captures all these factors.

A four-question reading test

  1. What is being measured? A surface, the air, or a heat-health indicator?
  2. Where and when? A sunny afternoon and a calm night can tell different stories.
  3. What is the comparison? Same instruments and conditions, or different datasets?
  4. Who is included? A neighborhood average can conceal meaningful exposure differences.

Shade remains important: trees can shield people and surfaces from direct sun, while vegetation also cools through evapotranspiration. The careful version of the claim is not “this shadow lowered the air temperature by a fixed amount.” It is that shade changes exposure to direct solar radiation, and trees can contribute to urban cooling through more than one pathway. Measuring the difference requires naming the outcome.
